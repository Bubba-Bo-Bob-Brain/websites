Foreword

Welcome, seeker, to this compendium of Slavic folklore. Within these pages you will find detailed accounts of the spirits, demons, and magical beings that inhabit the forests, rivers, and homes of the Slavic lands. These creatures have haunted the collective imagination for centuries, appearing in folktales, rituals, and warnings passed down through generations.

This bestiary has been compiled from ancient sources, ethnographic records, and oral traditions. Each entry includes a woodcut illustration, physical description, habits, powers, and methods of protection. Use this knowledge wisely—for to know a creature is to gain power over it, but also to invite its attention.

Beware! Many of these beings are dangerous, cunning, and hungry for human souls. Tread carefully through these pages, and may the old gods protect you.

The Chronicler of the Old Ways Compiled in the Year of the Oak

Leshy

Lord of the Forest

Taxonomy

  • Type: Nature Spirit / Forest Guardian
  • Habitat: Deep forests, ancient woods
  • Region: Slavic lands, especially Russia, Ukraine, Poland
  • Season: Most active in spring and summer

Physical Description

The Leshy appears as a towering humanoid figure, 7-10 feet tall, with skin the color of bark or moss. His most distinctive features are his antlers (like those of a stag) and his long, shaggy beard of lichen. He is covered in a fine layer of moss, lichen, and forest debris that serves as perfect camouflage. His eyes glow with an inner green light, and his feet are cloven like a goat's, though he can disguise them as human when needed. Some accounts say he has only one eye in the center of his forehead, like a cyclops.

Behavior & Powers

Leshy is the guardian of the forest and all its creatures. He knows every tree, every animal, and every hidden path. He can shapeshift into any forest animal or even a human, though he always retains some animal characteristic. He leads hunting parties, protects wildlife from harm, and punishes those who disrespect the forest. He can create illusions, summon storms, and cause travelers to become lost (sometimes for years). He is a trickster but not inherently evil—he respects those who respect the forest.

Weaknesses & Protection

Leshy can be repelled by making the sign of the cross (Christian influence) or by using fire—he fears flames. Wearing a sprig of rowan or carrying a piece of iron can offer protection. To gain his favor, leave offerings of bread, salt, or tobacco at the base of ancient trees. Never cut down a sacred tree or hunt more than you need—he will seek vengeance.

Folklore Tale

In one story, a hunter who got lost in the forest encountered a Leshy who offered to guide him home—but only after the hunter promised to never again hunt in that forest. When the hunter broke his promise, the Leshy led him into a thicket where he remained for seven years, believing only days had passed.

Rusalka

The Water Spirit

Taxonomy

  • Type: Water Spirit / Ghost
  • Habitat: Rivers, lakes, ponds, swamps
  • Region: Throughout Slavic lands
  • Season: Most active at night, especially during Rusalka Week (June)

Physical Description

Rusalka appears as a beautiful young woman with long, flowing hair that is either green or pale blonde. Her skin is pale, almost translucent, and her eyes are said to be like deep water—sometimes green, sometimes black, with no pupils. She is always naked, with water dripping from her hair and clothes (though she may wear a translucent shift). Her feet are reversed—her heels are in front and toes behind, a telltale sign of her supernatural nature. She can be recognized by the water droplets that perpetually fall from her fingertips.

Behavior & Powers

Rusalka are the spirits of women who died tragic deaths, often by drowning (suicide, murder, or accident). They haunt the waters where they died, luring men—especially handsome young ones—to their deaths with their beauty and singing. They can control water, create storms, and drown victims with a single touch. In some tales, they are not malicious but lonely, seeking companionship. They dance in moonlit meadows during Rusalka Week, and any man who joins them may be doomed to dance until he dies of exhaustion.

Weaknesses & Protection

Rusalka cannot cross running water if it is blessed or contains a cross. Holy symbols, iron, and rowan branches repel them. They fear fire and smoke. To protect children (whom they sometimes kidnap), one should place a piece of iron in the cradle or hang a garlic wreath. To banish a Rusalka, one must perform a funeral rite for her, giving her peace.

Folklore Tale

The most famous Rusalka story is the opera by Dvořák, but in folklore, there is the tale of a fisherman who was saved from a Rusalka's embrace when his wife threw a piece of bread (a holy food) into the water, breaking the Rusalka's power.

Baba Yaga

The Witch of the Forest

Taxonomy

  • Type: Witch / Ancient Crone
  • Habitat: Forest, in a hut on chicken legs
  • Region: Russia, Ukraine, Belarus, and beyond
  • Alignment: Chaotic neutral—tests and devours

Physical Description

Baba Yaga is depicted as a terrifying old woman with a long, bony nose, iron teeth, and wild gray hair. She is extremely thin, with legs that are like chicken bones. Her most iconic feature is her dwelling: a hut that stands on chicken legs (sometimes one leg, sometimes two) that can walk and turn its back to visitors. The hut is surrounded by a fence made of human bones, with skulls on top that glow with eerie light. She flies in a giant mortar, using a pestle as a rudder, and sweeps her tracks with a broom made of silver birch.

Behavior & Powers

Baba Yaga is a complex figure—she can be a terrifying ogress who eats children, but also a helpful witch who aids heroes with magical gifts. She tests those who come to her, setting impossible tasks. If they succeed, she rewards them with wisdom, magical items, or guidance. If they fail or show disrespect, she may eat them or turn them into animals. She is a guardian of the threshold between worlds, a keeper of ancient knowledge, and a mother figure to some spirits. She is associated with death, fertility, and transformation.

Weaknesses & Protection

Baba Yaga can be outwitted with cleverness and politeness. One must never refuse her requests outright but find a way to answer that satisfies her riddles. Holy water, crosses, and iron can harm her. To enter her hut, one must say "Hut, hut, turn your back to me and your front to the forest." To leave, one must grease the hinges with butter to prevent her from pursuing. Her power is tied to her hut—if the hut is destroyed or the legs are burned, she is greatly weakened.

Folklore Tale

In the tale of "Vasilisa the Beautiful," Baba Yaga sets Vasilisa impossible tasks (sorting poppy seeds, washing a black shirt white). With the help of a magical doll given by her mother, Vasilisa succeeds, and Baba Yaga rewards her with a skull that lights her way home, burning her wicked stepmother and stepsisters.

Zmey

The Three-Headed Dragon

Taxonomy

  • Type: Dragon / Serpent
  • Habitat: Mountains, caves, dragon's hills
  • Region: Slavic lands, especially Serbia, Bulgaria, Russia
  • Alignment: Malevolent, greedy, chaotic

Physical Description

The Zmey (also called Zmaj or Zmiy) is a massive dragon, typically with three heads (though sometimes seven or twelve). Each head has its own consciousness but shares a single body. The body is serpentine, often with wings (though some legends say they don't fly but use wings as shields), and covered in impenetrable scales. The heads can regrow if cut off unless the stump is burned or treated with fire. Zmey breathes fire, has claws that rend steel, and its tail can crush a house. Its size varies—some are as small as a horse, others as large as a hill. Some have human-like faces on their chests.

Behavior & Powers

Zmey are greedy treasure-hoarders, dwelling in caves filled with gold, jewels, and captured maidens. They love to kidnap beautiful women (sometimes princesses) and keep them as brides. They are intelligent, cunning, and speak human languages. They can shapeshift into humans or animals to deceive. They cause droughts, storms, and earthquakes when angered. They are often the antagonists in hero tales, requiring a brave knight to defeat them. Unlike Western dragons, Zmey are more serpent-like and less noble.

Weaknesses & Protection

Zmey's only weakness is fire—its heads must be burned after decapitation to prevent regrowth. It is vulnerable to magical weapons, especially those blessed by saints. Some tales say that a Zmey's heart can be stolen by a maiden who seduces it, rendering it powerless. To protect villages from Zmey attacks, people would perform rituals, carve protective symbols, and sacrifice animals or maidens (in older times).

Folklore Tale

The most famous Zmey tale is of Saint George and the Dragon, adapted in Slavic lands. In Serbian epic poetry, Zmaj Ognjeni Vuk (Fiery Dragon Zmey) is a heroic figure. In Bulgarian lore, the Zmey is both destroyer and protector—some villages believed their local Zmey protected them from invaders.

Vodyanoy

The Water Man

Taxonomy

  • Type: Water Spirit / Elemental
  • Habitat: Rivers, lakes, milldams, deep pools
  • Region: Throughout Slavic lands
  • Season: Most active in summer and during floods

Physical Description

Vodyanoy appears as a large, old man with a long green beard and hair, with skin that is blue-green and scaly like a fish. His eyes are yellow and protuberant, and he has gills on his neck. He is naked except for a belt of seaweed, and sometimes wears a crown of water lilies. He carries a club or fishing rod, and rides on a catfish or pike. He can shapeshift into a fish, a log, or a handsome youth to lure victims. In some regions, he is depicted with a bloated, drowned appearance, with water constantly dripping from his body.

Behavior & Powers

Vodyanoy is the ruler of all water bodies and their creatures. He controls fish populations, can cause floods or droughts, and drowns those who disrespect the water (swearing, polluting, fishing without offerings). He is a trickster who enjoys playing pranks: hiding fishing nets, turning boats around, making fish leap into nets unexpectedly. He can be helpful if propitiated—fishermen leave offerings of tobacco, bread, or vodka to ensure good catches. He sometimes captures humans to be his servants or brides. He has a family: a wife (Vodyanitsa) and children who are also water spirits.

Weaknesses & Protection

Vodyanoy cannot cross thresholds (a common Slavic spirit limitation). Holy symbols, iron, and rowan branches repel him. To protect against drowning, one should make the sign of the cross before entering deep water. Fishermen must offer the first catch to Vodyanoy by throwing it back while thanking him. He fears fire and cannot survive out of water for long. If captured, he can be forced to grant wishes or reveal secrets, but he will seek revenge if released.

Folklore Tale

A miller who neglected to leave offerings for Vodyanoy found his mill dammed with reeds and his water wheels broken. When he finally left a gift of bread and salt, the next morning the dam was cleared and the water flowed freely. Some say Vodyanoy still haunts that mill to this day.

Domovoi

The House Spirit

Taxonomy

  • Type: Household Spirit
  • Habitat: Homes, especially under the stove or in the barn
  • Region: All Slavic lands
  • Alignment: Generally benevolent, protective

Physical Description

Domovoi appears as a small, hairy man, about 2-3 feet tall, with a long beard and unkempt hair. His skin is rough and bark-like, and he is covered in coarse hair. He has eyes that glow like embers. He is often naked but sometimes wears a tiny shirt and trousers. He may appear as a familiar animal (cat, dog, mouse) or as a miniature version of the household patriarch. He is always associated with a specific home and cannot leave it unless the family moves.

Behavior & Powers

Domovoi is the guardian of the household. He helps with chores, tends livestock, protects the family from harm, and warns of impending danger (by banging on walls, moving objects, or appearing in dreams). He punishes lazy or disrespectful family members—pinching, throwing things, or causing mischief. He rewards kindness with small gifts (coins, food) and extra help. He is especially fond of children and may play with them. He must be fed regularly (a bowl of milk, bread, or porridge left on the stove). If neglected or insulted, he may leave, bringing misfortune.

Weaknesses & Protection

Domovoi cannot cross thresholds without invitation. He fears cleanliness and order—too much sweeping or washing may drive him away (some say he hides in the dirt under the stove). Loud noises, swearing, and disrespect anger him. To keep him happy: leave offerings, speak respectfully, maintain a clean but not spotless home, and involve him in family decisions. If he leaves, one can call him back by leaving the door open and saying "Grandfather Domovoi, come home."

Folklore Tale

A farmer's Domovoi saved the family's cattle by appearing as a huge cat and scaring off a wolf. In return, the farmer left an extra bowl of milk each night. When a new wife mocked the idea of a house spirit, the Domovoi left, and the next day the barn burned down—the family learned the hard way to respect their guardian.

Bannik

The Bath Spirit

Taxonomy

  • Type: Bath Spirit / Steam Demon
  • Habitat: Saunas, bathhouses (banya)
  • Region: Russia, Finland, Baltic regions
  • Season: Active year-round, especially in winter

Physical Description

Bannik appears as a small, naked old man with a long white beard, covered in soot and steam. His skin is red from the heat, and he is often depicted with a soot-blackened body and glowing eyes. He carries a bundle of birch branches (venik) used for beating in the sauna. He may have claws or long fingers. In some accounts, he is covered in a layer of steam that obscures his features. He is associated with the extreme heat and humidity of the banya, and his presence is felt in the swirling steam and the scent of birch.

Behavior & Powers

Bannik is the guardian of the bathhouse, a place of purification and healing in Slavic tradition. He tests those who enter the banya, especially women (as banyas were often women's domains). If respected, he can grant healing, beauty, and fertility. If disrespected (by swearing, fighting, or improper conduct), he may scald, suffocate, or drive bathers mad. He can predict the future—answers to questions asked in the steam are believed to come from him. He is protective of children born in the banya and may steal unbaptized babies if offended.

Weaknesses & Protection

Bannik respects cleanliness and ritual. Proper conduct in the banya—no swearing, no fighting, respect for the space—keeps him appeased. Holy symbols, prayers, and making the sign of the cross can repel him if he becomes threatening. To avoid his wrath, one must never insult the banya, never point fingers (considered rude), and always leave a portion of the first dipper of water for him. If he becomes angry, throwing a handful of salt into the fire can drive him away.

Folklore Tale

In one story, a woman who used the banya for witchcraft summoned Bannik, who appeared as a giant steam cloud. She offered him a sacrifice of bread and salt, and he taught her healing spells. When she used them for harm, Bannik returned and filled the banya with such scalding steam that she fled, never to return.

Vila

The Mountain Fairy

Taxonomy

  • Type: Nature Spirit / Fairy
  • Habitat: Mountains, forests, meadows, near water
  • Region: South Slavic lands (Serbia, Croatia, Bulgaria)
  • Alignment: Chaotic neutral—beautiful but dangerous

Physical Description

Vila are incredibly beautiful women with long, flowing hair (often golden or white) and radiant skin. They are tall and slender, with eyes like stars. They wear shimmering, translucent gowns made of moonbeams and dew, and have wings like dragonflies or butterflies (though some tales say they fly without wings). They are often seen dancing in circles in the moonlight, leaving behind circular patches of flattened grass (fairy rings). Their voices are enchanting, and their laughter sounds like crystal bells. They are sometimes depicted riding on stags or wolves.

Behavior & Powers

Vila are guardians of the natural world, especially mountains and wild places. They protect animals, heal the sick, and aid lost travelers—if they deem the person worthy. They can be mischievous, leading travelers astray, kidnapping young men to be their husbands, or stealing children (leaving changelogs). They are skilled in magic, healing, and prophecy. They hate cruelty, arrogance, and those who harm nature. They are associated with spring, fertility, and the dawn. Their breath can heal or curse, and their tears have magical properties.

Weaknesses & Protection

Vila can be repelled by iron, rowan, and holy symbols. They cannot cross running water if it is blessed. To protect against kidnapping, one should wear a piece of iron or a protective charm. To gain their favor, leave offerings of honey, milk, or flowers in wild places. Never refuse their dance invitation—it's considered a great honor, but one must be careful not to dance too long, as time flows differently in their realm. If a human falls in love with a Vila, it often ends in tragedy.

Folklore Tale

The Vila are central to the Serbian epic poem "The Death of Mother Jugović's Children," where nine Vila (sisters) mourn the fallen heroes and carry their souls to the afterlife. In Croatian lore, the Vila of the Velebit mountain are said to appear on full moon nights, dancing and singing, and anyone who sees them is granted a wish—but must never speak of it.

Upyr

The Slavic Vampire

Taxonomy

  • Type: Undead / Revenant
  • Habitat: Graves, crypts, abandoned buildings
  • Region: Russia, Ukraine, Serbia, Bulgaria
  • Alignment: Malevolent, predatory

Physical Description

The Upyr (also spelled Upir) is a Slavic vampire that differs from the Eastern European vampire of later folklore. It appears as a recently deceased person, but with some unsettling features: a bloated, ruddy or dark purple corpse-like appearance, long fingernails and hair that continues to grow after death, and a gaping mouth filled with sharp teeth. It may have a single eye in the center of its forehead (like a cyclops) or two glowing eyes. It can shapeshift into a bat, wolf, or mist. Unlike later vampires, Upyrs are not necessarily undead—they can be living witches or sorcerers who have gained vampiric powers.

Behavior & Powers

Upyrs rise from the grave at night to feed on the blood of the living, especially family members and livestock. They can also suffocate victims by sitting on their chest (the "mare" aspect). They are intelligent and vengeful, often targeting those who wronged them in life. They can cast curses, control animals (especially wolves and owls), and create storms. Some Upyrs are not evil but are compelled to feed due to improper burial or a curse. They are strongest at midnight and during the new moon. They cannot cross thresholds without invitation, cannot see their reflection, and are repelled by sunlight, fire, and holy symbols.

Weaknesses & Protection

Upyrs can be killed by decapitation, staking through the heart (preferably with a thorn or aspen stake), burning the body, or pouring holy water on the grave. Garlic, rowan, and wolfsbane repel them. To prevent someone from becoming an Upyr: bury the body face-down, place a sickle or coin in the mouth, tie the hands and feet, and scatter poppy seeds or sand around the grave (they must count every seed, keeping them occupied until dawn). Mirrors should be placed on the grave to prevent the Upyr from rising. Exorcism prayers can also banish them.

Folklore Tale

The most famous Upyr story is that of the Serbian vampire Sava Savanović, who lived in a watermill and drank the blood of millers. He was eventually killed when a miller lured him with a plow and impaled him. Similar tales exist across Russia, where Upyrs were blamed for sudden deaths and epidemics.

Nav

The Soul of the Dead

Taxonomy

  • Type: Soul / Spirit of the Dead
  • Habitat: Graves, crossroads, abandoned places
  • Region: South Slavic lands (Serbia, Croatia, Bulgaria)
  • Status: Unresting dead, often improperly buried

Physical Description

Nav (plural: Navi) is the soul of a dead person that has not found peace. It appears as a translucent, ethereal figure resembling the deceased, but often with features reversed (feet forward, head backward) or with a completely featureless face. It may appear as a floating white cloth, a shadow, or a glowing orb. In some tales, Navi are the souls of stillborn babies or unbaptized children. They are always pale, glowing faintly, and make no sound—or produce an eerie whistling. They can be seen only at night or during specific times (like Navi Day, a Slavic equivalent of Halloween).

Behavior & Powers

Navi are restless spirits that wander the earth, often near their graves or places of death. They can cause illness, nightmares, and sudden death by touching or breathing on victims. They are particularly dangerous to pregnant women and children. They may return to their families, moaning and weeping, seeking to be buried properly. Some Navi are protective of their descendants, but most are sorrowful and vengeful. They can possess the living, especially those who are weak-willed or grieving. They are associated with the underworld and the goddess Morana (goddess of death).

Weaknesses & Protection

Navi cannot cross running water or pass over crossroads if a cross is placed there. Holy symbols, prayers, and proper burial rites can lay them to rest. To protect against Navi, one should place crosses on graves, burn incense, and perform memorial services (Radonitsa). Iron and rowan are effective. If a Nav appears, one should not look directly at it, should make the sign of the cross, and should speak firmly, commanding it to leave. Proper funeral rites—including washing the body, dressing it in white, and placing coins for the afterlife—prevent someone from becoming a Nav.

Folklore Tale

In Serbian tradition, Navi are especially active during the week before Easter ( Passion Week). People avoid going out at night, leave food on graves, and perform rituals to appease the dead. The most famous Nav legend is that of the "Navi of the Crossroads," who appears to travelers and asks riddles; answering incorrectly brings doom.
