Godfrey d'Armand

⚔️ The Fallen Knight

Once a proud chevalier of the Order of the Iron Cross, Godfrey was stripped of his titles when he refused to burn a village accused of spreading the pestilence. Now he wanders the ravaged countryside, sword in hand, seeking redemption through bloodshed he deems righteous.

Godfrey was born the second son of Count Armand of Burgundy, never inheriting the family keep. He joined the Crusades at seventeen and returned twenty years later — a changed man, his faith fractured but his blade arm steady. When the Black Death reached his lord's territory, Godfrey defied a direct order to torch the infected village of Montjoie, saving over sixty lives but earning the wrath of his liege. Branded a traitor and stripped of his spurs, he rode into the night with nothing but his sword and his horse.

He has since wandered the plague-ridden lands, offering protection to beleaguered villages in exchange for food and shelter. His moral code is strict: he will not harm innocents, but he shows no mercy to those who prey upon the weak during the apocalypse. Despite his gruff exterior, Godfrey harbors a deep tenderness — especially for children orphaned by the plague.

Marguerite Voisin

💀 The Plague Doctor

Armed with her iconic beaked mask and a satchel of dubious remedies, Marguerite tends to the dying across the plague-stricken provinces. She has seen more death in one season than most see in a lifetime — and it has not broken her, but honed her resolve into something terrifying and absolute.

Marguerite was born to a family of physicians in Avignon, studying under the finest minds of the Montpellier school. When the plague arrived, she did not flee — she rolled up her sleeves and filled her beak-mask with lavender and camphor. Her methods are unconventional: she combines Galenic medicine with folk remedies she learned from village wise women. Some call her a saint. Others whisper that she has made pacts to survive what kills everyone around her.

Her faith is her anchor. She prays before each patient, and she keeps a leather-bound journal in which she records every death — name, age, symptoms, last words. She has filled eleven journals so far.

They say she was born with the caul over her face. They say she speaks to crows and commands the roots of ancient oaks. Since the plague came, the villagers who once cursed her as a witch now leave offerings at the edge of her forest — desperate prayers whispered into the dark.

Hilda was once a village midwife, married to a woodcutter and mother to three. When the fever took her children and then her husband, the village turned on her. They dragged her before the parish priest and accused her of consorting with the Devil. She escaped the pyre by fleeing into the deep forest where no Christian man would follow.

Now she lives in a crooked hut woven from living branches, attended by a pack of half-wolf dogs. She knows every root, fungus, and herb for fifty leagues around. The plague does not touch her — or if it does, she heals herself with brews that would make a monk vomit. She is neither good nor evil; she is the forest's will given human shape.

Brother Anselm von Köln

📖 The Penitent Monk

Anselm has not spoken in forty days. He walks the roads between shattered monasteries, copying what manuscripts survive and burying the dead with prayers no one else will say. His silence is both penance and prophecy — he believes God has taken his voice so his hands may do the speaking.

Born to a Köln merchant family, Anselm showed piety from infancy — or so the monks at St. Pantaleon claimed. He entered the Benedictine order at twelve and spent twenty years in scriptorium silence before the plague broke the monastery like a twig under a boot. Every brother died. Every brother except Anselm.

He took this survival as a sign: God has work for him. He walked out into the dying world with a cart full of blank parchment and a heart full of terrible purpose. He buries strangers. He copies scripture. He has not spoken since the day he dug a mass grave for sixty children outside Nürnberg.

Ysolde Blacktide

🪦 The Grave Digger

Ysolde digs graves before dawn and drinks before noon. She has buried more people this year than she can count — and she stopped counting at four hundred. There is a darkness in her that the plague only deepened, not created. She knows every corpse by name, and she visits her favorites at night.

Ysolde's mother was a tavern wench and her father, she claimed, was Death himself. She grew up in the graveyard of a small Franconian village, playing among the stones as other children played in meadows. At fifteen, she took over the grave-digging duties from the old sexton who dropped dead mid-prayer.

The plague made her wealthy, in a way. Families paid handsomely for proper burials. Ysolde buried them all — peasant and lord, child and crone. She speaks to the dead as casually as others speak to the living. Whether this is madness or holiness, she refuses to say.

Konrad's family has manned the village smithy for four generations. His arms are thick as oak trunks, his hands calloused beyond recognition. When the plague came, people needed nails to board their doors, blades to defend their homes, and hinges for the makeshift coffins. Konrad works from dawn to midnight, eating what Cunigunde places before him and speaking only when spoken to.

He suspects the Church is responsible for the plague — or at least profiting from it. He will not say this aloud. He lets his hammer do the talking, one ringing blow at a time.

Elara was born eyeless in a ditch outside the walls of Metz. A tinker raised her until he sold her to a troupe of mendicant friars who put her to work as a living relic — "the Blind Oracle." They took her from town to town, and the credulous paid to hear her babble. But somewhere in her twentieth year, the babbling became prophecy.

She foresaw the plague. She told her masters. They beat her and moved on. When death followed her warning, she was no longer a curiosity but a terror. Now she walks alone, wrapped in rags that smell of sulfur and sage, warning anyone who will listen. The question that haunts everyone: is she truly touched by God, or has the plague addled what little mind she has?

Lucien was born the bastard son of a minor count in Picardy. The Church was his only path to power, and he climbed it with the ruthlessness of a siege engine. By thirty, he was dean of Mortagne. By thirty-five, the prior was "conveniently" dead.

The plague is Lucien's greatest opportunity. Desperate souls will pay anything for hope. He sells indulgences, blessed amulets, and "holy" water at prices that would make a usurer blush. He has accumulated a fortune in gold, jewels, and relics — most of which are fakes. He does not believe in God. He believes in leverage.

Now she travels with a donkey laden with salvaged ingredients, moving from settlement to settlement. She treats the sick without payment. Godfrey, who owes a debt to her late father, watches over her with a devotion he would never admit to.

Dietrich was a journeyman butcher in Strasbourg when the plague took him. He collapsed in the market square, nose bleeding, skin black with buboes. The parish collected his body and threw him into a pit with eleven others. Three days later, he woke — screaming, clawing, covered in the filth of the dead.

The villagers wanted to burn him as a revenant. But Father Lucien, ever the pragmatist, declared him blessed by God and paraded him through the streets. Dietrich says nothing about what he saw in those three days underground. He only says: "Death is not the end of suffering."

Cunigunde has delivered over two hundred children in her forty-seven years. She has seen mothers die with the child still half-born. She has baptized infants with her own trembling hands when no priest would come. She has smothered one newborn to save the mother — and never forgiven herself.

Her husband Konrad keeps her fed and sheltered, but she spends more nights with laboring mothers than in her own bed. She learned old midwife secrets from Hilda, the forest witch, and combines them with prayers to the Virgin. When the plague came, she chose to stay while others fled. "Someone must bring the children into this world," she says, "even a world like this."

Orphaned at six during the last famine, Reynard survived by stealing. He was caught, branded, and sent to the mines outside Freiburg. He escaped during an earthquake that killed four guards. Now he moves between plague-ravaged towns like a fox through a henhouse, always one step ahead of the bailiff.

He tells himself he only takes from the dead — but the living have noticed missing valuables too. Ysolde knows his routes and sometimes joins him. They split the take and share a bottle of bad wine afterward. Reynard does not fear God, the plague, or the hangman. He fears only one thing: dying poor.
