FEATURE

The Grand Hotel — A Monument to Modern Ambition

By Reginald Beaumont • 12 min read

Rising forty-two stories above the boulevard, the Grand Hotel stands as the crown jewel of the new metropolitan skyline. Its terraced ziggurat silhouette — inspired by the ancient temples of Mesopotamia — catches the morning light in bands of gold and shadow. Inside, every surface gleams: black marble floors polished to a mirror finish, geometric ceiling panels inlaid with gold leaf, and art deco sconces that cast warm amber light across corridors lined with mahogany.

The hotel's grand ballroom, with its sweeping staircase and mirrored walls, has already become the most sought-after venue for the season's most extravagant affairs. "It is not merely a building," declares architect Lucien Marchand, "it is a declaration — that we have entered an era of unapologetic magnificence."

EDITORIAL

Women of the Jazz Age: Notes on Liberation

By Clementine Ashford • 8 min read

There is a woman at every table in every speakeasy from here to Harlem, and she is not asking permission. She rolls her stockings to the knee, slicks her hair with brilliantine, and draws on her lips a colour that would have scandalized the matrons of yesterday. The flapper is not a phase — she is a revolution in sequins.

Fashion has followed, naturally. The chemise dress, dropped at the waist and fitted close through the hip, moves like water and permits like nothing. Women smoke in public. Women drive. Women own property. And the world, for all its gasping, has not ended — it has merely become more interesting.

SOCIETY

The Vanderbilts' Spring Cotillion

By Penelope Rothwell • 6 min read

Last Thursday evening, the Vanderbilt estate played host to what is being called the social event of the decade. The spring cotillion drew three hundred of the city's most prominent figures to a garden pavilion adorned with thousands of fresh peonies and climbing white roses. The orchestra — imported at tremendous expense from Copenhagen — performed until three in the morning.

The evening's pièce de résistance was the unveiling of Mrs. Vanderbilt's new jewellery collection: a matching set of diamond and emerald pieces designed by Cartier, featuring geometric motifs inspired by ancient Egyptian art. "Every stone tells a story," Mrs. Vanderbilt remarked, adjusting an emerald clip at her collar. "These tell the story of an age that knows no limits."

ART & CULTURE

Art Deco: Geometry as Gospel

By Hector Delacroix • 10 min read

The new aesthetic sweeping through every medium — from architecture to jewellery, from furniture to fashion — shares a single, unifying principle: the supremacy of the geometric form. The sunburst, the chevron, the stepped silhouette, the fan motif — these are not mere decorations. They are the visual language of a civilization that has decided the future shall be beautiful, and beautiful in a very particular way: bold, symmetrical, and unapologetically ornate.

We are witnessing the birth of a new style that rejects the fussiness of the Victorian age in favour of clean lines and bold contrasts. Gold against black. Cream against navy. The result is an art form that feels both ancient and futuristic — a style for the machine age that refuses to abandon the romance of the past.
