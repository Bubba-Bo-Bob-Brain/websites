The Age of Excess

Inside the Gilded Palaces of Long Island's New Aristocracy

By Eleanor Vanderbilt

SOCIETY PAGES

The Mansions of Millionaire's Mile

Where the new money flows like champagne at a Wall Street victory party, the estates of Long Island have become monuments to an era of unprecedented prosperity.

There is a peculiar magic to the North Shore of Long Island, where the salty air of the Sound mingles with the intoxicating perfume of wealth. Here, along what wits have dubbed "Millionaire's Mile," the industrial princes of America have raised temples to their own success—great sprawling mansions of limestone and marble, their windows glittering like diamonds against the twilight.

❧
"We live in a golden age, my dear friends—a time when dreams are minted into reality and the impossible has become merely improbable."
❧

The parties at these estates have become the stuff of legend. At Harbor's Edge, the home of railroad magnate Harrison Blackwell III, the festivities begin at midnight and continue until dawn breaks over the water. Orchestra players imported from Paris play until their fingers bleed, and the champagne flows in quantities that would make a French vineyard blush.

Yet beneath the gilded surface, there are whispers of trouble in paradise. The older families—those whose fortunes date back to the Civil War and before—watch with growing unease as the new aristocracy plays at sophistication with all the subtlety of a brass band. "Money," observes society matron Mrs. Cornelius Van Derlyn, with a knowing tilt of her bejeweled head, "can buy many things. Taste, unfortunately, is not among them."

◇
The New Titans

The names on everyone's lips this season tell the story of American industry transformed. The petroleum kings, the automobile wizards, the speculators who turned a bull market into personal empires—they are the new lords of society, and they wear their crowns with defiant pride.

At the pinnacle sits Charles "Copper" Montgomery, whose mines in Arizona have yielded a fortune that would make Croesus weep with envy. His new estate, provisionally named "Copper Crest," rises on three hundred acres of prime waterfront, its copper-domed towers visible for miles across the Sound.
