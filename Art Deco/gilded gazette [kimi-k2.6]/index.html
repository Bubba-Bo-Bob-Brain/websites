The Last Great Party

For one shimmering evening, the Van der Luyden estate became the center of the known universe—and perhaps signaled its end

By Dorothy Whitmore-Parkinson · June 15, 1927

It began, as all significant things do, with a rumor. whispered in the powder room of the Ritz, murmured between hands at the Colony Club, insinuated into the ear of Winchell himself over oysters at the Stork Club. The Van der Luydens were giving a party. Not merely a party—a fête. A spectacle. An apotheosis of everything the age had come to represent, distilled into a single night of phosphorescent madness.

By nine o'clock on Saturday, the motorcars were backed up along Oyster Bay for nearly two miles. Isotta-Fraschinis, Hispano-Suizas, Rolls-Royce Silver Ghosts gleaming like votive objects in the June dusk. The women emerged in clouds of Chanel No. 5 and Schiaparelli shocking pink, their heads bound in jeweled bandeaux that caught the last light like the coronets of minor royalty. The men in white tie and tails, some already showing the telltale flush of the hip flask, the brave ones in the new dinner jackets that old Mr. Van der Luyden still refused to acknowledge as proper.

❞
It was as if Versailles had been transported to Long Island, and Marie Antoinette had learned to Charleston.
❞
— Ogden Nash, observed from the azalea bushes

The house itself had been transformed. Where the great hall usually displayed Mrs. Van der Luyden's collection of Dutch masters, now hung cascades of jasmine and gardenia, the air so thick with their perfume that one felt almost drunk upon entering. The fountain in the central court ran not with water but with champagne—Dom Pérignon 1921, I was reliably informed by a young man from the Tatler who had taste-tested it with scientific solemnity. Four hundred cases, chilled to exactly seven degrees Celsius.

The Guest of Honor Who Never Arrived

We had been promised, through the elaborate machinery of social semaphore that governs such occasions, the presence of a certain gentleman recently returned from Paris. A novelist of some reputation, though his books were more discussed than read in the circles that matter. His failure to appear—whether through discretion, indisposition, or the more prosaic obstacle of his current location in the south of France—did nothing to diminish the evening. If anything, his absence lent it the frisson of the forbidden, the sense that we were celebrating something that could not quite be named.

The Charleston Contest, judged by Mr. Gershwin at the piano

At midnight, the orchestra—imported at considerable expense from the Savoy in London—struck up Rhapsody in Blue, and the dancing proper began. I watched Mrs. Reginald Vanderbilt execute a series of movements that would have caused her mother-in-law to reach for the smelling salts, while a young man in patent leather pumps performed a backflip that scattered caviar across three yards of Aubusson carpet. No one seemed to mind. The rules, such as they were, had been suspended for the evening, packed away with the Dutch masters and the good silver.

The night wore on in the manner of such nights, gaining momentum even as it lost coherence. Somewhere around two, I found myself in conversation with a Brazilian aviator who spoke no English and whose French was worse than mine, yet we understood each other perfectly on the subject of absinthe and the essential loneliness of flight. At three, the fireworks began—fifteen thousand dollars' worth of pyrotechnics, according to the Times man, who had obtained the figure from the butler with the ease of long practice.

The Morning After

I departed at dawn, walking the two miles to the station because every motorcar had been claimed by those less prudent in their consumption of the fountain's offerings. The sky was the color of a bruised pearl, and the dew on the Long Island grass was heavy as mercury. Behind me, the house still blazed with light, though the music had given way to the more intimate sounds of a party winding down: broken glass, sudden laughter, the occasional splash of someone testing whether the fountain still ran true.

Walking, I found myself wondering—perhaps it was the champagne, perhaps the hour—whether we had witnessed something final. The last convulsion of an age that had begun in the trenches and would end, surely, in something equally unthinkable. The last great party before the music stopped. It was a morbid thought, unworthy of the morning, and I dismissed it as the residue of too little sleep and too much Dom Pérignon.

But I have not forgotten the look on old Mr. Van der Luyden's face, glimpsed briefly as he stood alone on the terrace, watching his guests disport themselves in his fountain of champagne. Something in his expression—not disapproval, exactly, but a species of melancholy recognition, as if he understood that all this magnificence was borrowed against a debt he would not live to pay.

The sun rose gold over the Sound. The motorcars began their long crawl back to the city. And somewhere, I am certain, the planning began for next Saturday's entertainment, because that is the nature of such things, and because we were all of us—we are all of us—incapable of imagining any other way to live.
