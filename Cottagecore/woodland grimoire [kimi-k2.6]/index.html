Morrow-Dream Wine

🌑 New Moon

"Steeped when the moon hides her face. Brings visions of paths not yet walked, truths not yet spoken. The sleeper wakes with dew upon their lips and certainty in their heart."

Ingredients

  • 3 sprigs Dried mugwort, harvested before dawn
  • 1 thimble Honey from wild lavender
  • 7 drops Essence of valerian root
  • A pinch Ground pearl mushroom

Method

  1. Crush mugwort between palms, speaking your question aloud.
  2. Steep in spring water never touched by iron, for thirteen minutes.
  3. Strain through cheesecloth woven by moonlight.
  4. Add honey while stirring widdershins, then valerian while stirring sunwise.
  5. Drink before sleep on the night of the new moon.
⚠

Do not brew when Mars is in retrograde. Dreams may become too vivid to wake from.

Heartsease Cordial

🌕 Full Moon

"For sorrow that has no name, for grief that lingers like winter fog. This cordial does not banish sadness but walks beside it, holding a lantern."

Ingredients

  • 1 handful Fresh heartsease flowers
  • 2 cups Rose petal tea, cooled
  • 1 spoon Motherwort tincture
  • 3 petals Borage flower, for courage

Method

  1. Gather heartsease at noon, when the sun warms their faces.
  2. Float flowers in rose tea, cover with linen, and let rest in a south-facing window for three days.
  3. On the full moon, add motherwort and borage.
  4. Bottle in violet glass, seal with beeswax.
☽

Best shared with one who understands silence.

Thornward Bound

🌓 Quarter Moon

"Protection woven from what protects itself. Blackberry brambles know the art of boundaries; this brew borrows their wisdom."

Ingredients

  • 1 arm's length Blackberry cane, cut with silver
  • 9 leaves Stinging nettle, gathered with gloves of leather
  • 1 kernel Rowan berry, dried three winters
  • Ash from Your own hearth-fire

Method

  1. Bind blackberry cane into a circle while speaking your intention.
  2. Burn nettle leaves to ash, mix with hearth-ash.
  3. Grind rowan berry with mortar of river stone.
  4. Combine at the quarter moon, when the world balances between light and dark.
  5. Sprinkle at thresholds, carry in a pouch of red wool.
⚠

Never use against the innocent, or the thorns will find your own threshold.

Starlight Tincture

✧ Any Clear Night

"For seeing what is hidden, for knowing what the stars have always known. Not all wisdom is meant for daylight eyes."

Ingredients

  • 1 cup Dew collected from yarrow leaves
  • 3 threads Saffron, each measured by a spider's thread
  • A whisper Of your true name
  • Starlight Caught in clear quartz for one turning

Method

  1. Place dew in vessel of blown glass, never fired in kiln.
  2. Add saffron at midnight; watch the color bloom like a second sunset.
  3. Hold quartz to your lips, speak your name so only you can hear.
  4. Let sit where starlight falls, for as many nights as you have years.
  5. One drop upon the tongue opens the inner eye.
☆

Some sights, once seen, cannot be unseen. Be certain of your courage.

Foxglove

"The folk's glove, the fairies' cap,
Purple bells that overlap,
Deadly beauty, healing art,
Two edges on a single heart."

Properties & Lore

Grows where the fairy folk have danced, or so the old ones say. The flowers nod downward, as if listening to secrets in the earth. Every part contains digitalin, a heart medicine so potent that the difference between cure and poison is measured in careful drops.

In the village where I was born, they planted foxglove near the door to keep wicked spirits from entering. I plant it for the bumblebees, who crawl so far into each bell that only their fuzzy bottoms show.

Known Uses

  • Tincture of leaf for failing heart (physician's measure only)
  • Compress of flower for deep bruises and swelling
  • Dried leaves in sachet for protection during sleep
  • Flower essence for those who fear they have no right to take up space

Springtide Gatherings

March Equinox — Beltane

🌿

Nettle Urtica dioica

Young shoots before flowering. Wear gloves of courage and thick cloth. The sting that burns becomes the soup that nourishes. Dry for tea, or wilt like spinach with garlic and good butter.

Damp hedgerows, abandoned pastures
🌸

Elderflower Sambucus nigra

Cream umbels heavy with scent, gathered on dry mornings before the sun grows hot. The tree that gives and gives—flower, berry, bark, wood. Ask permission before cutting; leave an offering of song or bread.

Woodland edges, stream banks
🍃

Wild Garlic Allium ursinum

The bear's leek, carpeting forest floors with white stars and pungent green. Follow your nose to find it. Leaves before flowers for potency, flowers for beauty in salad and vinegar.

Ancient woodlands, shaded slopes
🌼

Dandelion Taraxacum officinale

No weed, but a weed-wife's first friend. Root roasted for coffee, leaves for bitter tonic, flowers for wine that tastes of sunlight stored in golden form. The whole plant is medicine for those who have forgotten how to rest.

Everywhere the sun reaches

Midsummer Bounty

Beltane — Lammas

🌹

Dog Rose Rosa canina

Pink five-petaled simplicity, the wild rose of hedgerow and thicket. Petals for syrup and scent, hips for winter's vitamin fire. Gather petals when dew still clings, before the day's heat steals their perfume.

Hedgerows, scrubland, old railway lines
🌾

Mugwort Artemisia vulgaris

Silvery beneath, dark green above, with a scent that opens doors in the mind. The traveler herb, the dream herb, the herb of Artemis who needs no man. Harvest on St. John's Eve for greatest power.

Waste ground, river paths, old roads
🫐

Bilberry Vaccinium myrtillus

On hands and knees in heather, stained fingers and purple tongue. The fruit of patience, of slowing down, of noticing what grows low. Eat as many as you gather; the mountain demands its share.

High moorland, acidic soils
🌻

St. John's Wort Hypericum perforatum

Perforated leaves, golden flowers with five petals and a profusion of stamens. Oil made from these blooms turns ruby red, the color of captured sunlight. For darkness of spirit, for wounds that need light to heal.

Grassy places, open woodland

Harvest Hoarding

Lammas — Samhain

🍄

Chanterelle Cantharellus cibarius

Golden trumpets with false gills that run down the stem, smelling of apricots in the forest damp. The mushroom that even beginners may eat without fear. Sauté in butter, preserve in oil, dry for winter's golden memory.

Beneath beech and oak, mossy ground
🌰

Sweet Chestnut Castanea sativa

Spiny burrs splitting to reveal glossy brown jewels. Roast over fire, boil and mash, or candy with patience and syrup. The bread tree of old, feeding multitudes from a single ancient trunk.

Parkland, old estates, southern slopes
🍇

Hawthorn Berry Crataegus monogyna

Deep red haws, the food of birds and desperate winters. The may-tree's second gift, after its fleeting white blossom. For the heart that beats irregular, for the memory of May's brief glory.

Hedgerows, boundaries, old field edges
🧅

Wild Garlic Bulb Allium ursinum

The underground treasure, potent and pungent, gathered when leaves have withered. Smaller than garden garlic, but with a sweetness and complexity that cultivated bulbs lack. Dry in braids, or pickle in vinegar with spices.

Where spring leaves grew, now marked by seed heads

Yuletide Quiet

Samhain — March Equinox

🌲

Pine Needle Pinus sylvestris

Evergreen in the sleeping world, needles bundled in twos or fives. Rich in vitamin C, tasting of cold air and resin. Tea for the scurvy-months, steam for congested lungs, branches for the ritual fire that welcomes sun's return.

High forests, plantations, shelter belts
🌿

Ivy Leaf Hedera helix

Dark gloss in frost, the climber that holds green when all else surrenders. Not for eating, but for wreathing, for binding, for the magic of persistence. Associated with both Bacchus and the Christ-child—drink and divinity both.

Old walls, tree trunks, any vertical surface
🍄

Velvet Shank Flammulina velutipes

Orange caps on dark velvety stems, pushing through snow and frozen leaf-litter. The winter mushroom, the cold-weather friend. Mild in flavor, welcome for freshness when the larder grows strange with preserved things.

Dead elm, ash, and other hardwoods
🌹

Rose Hip Rosa canina

The dog rose's autumn gift, now softened by frost and ready for harvest. Cut in half, scoop seeds, dry slowly. Syrup for children's health, tea for winter's color, memories of pink summer in every cup.

Where summer roses bloomed, hips now hang

Remedy Notes

Scribbled in haste, smudged with sap, stained with the work of healing

For the Aching Head Tuesday last

The Head That Will Not Rest

Old Mathilde came again with her temples bound in cloth, the same cloth she wore when first I met her, now faded to the color of weak tea. "The knocking," she says, "the knocking behind my eyes."

I gave her:

  • Willow bark, shaved fine, steeped to bitterness
  • Meadowsweet flower, for the stomach the willow troubles
  • A few drops of lavender oil, rubbed at the temples

But also I told her: the knocking is your body asking you to stop. The body is honest when we are not. She wept. I made her chamomile with honey. She stayed until the rain stopped.

Note: Collect more meadowsweet. The marsh where it grows is being drained for houses.

For the Sleepless Midsummer's Eve

When Sleep Has Fled

The blacksmith's boy, too young for such worry, eyes like bruised fruit. His mother brings him at midnight, the only hour he is calm enough to travel. "He fears the dark," she whispers, though he is thirteen and should have outgrown such things.

I do not tell her that I too fear the dark, that I light candles against it even now, writing by their wavering.

For him I made:

  • Passionflower, the intricate bloom that looks like a clockwork of green and purple
  • Hops pillow, stuffed with the same that brews beer, but here for dreams not intoxication
  • A story, spoken low, of a boy who befriended the dark and found it full of moths

He slept on my cot, his mother in the chair. I watched them both, envying their surrender.

Note: The passionflower vine has reached the roof. Must trim before it finds the chimney.

For the Broken Heart Autumn Equinox

The Heart's Unmending

She did not come herself. Her sister brought the request, eyes averted, as if heartache were shameful. "She will not rise from bed. She will not eat. It has been six weeks since he left for the city and did not return."

I prepared:

  • Hawthorn berry tincture, for the heart that literally falters
  • Motherwort, the lion's tail, for courage named for maternal strength
  • Rose petal honey, for sweetness that does not depend on another's presence

But I sent also a letter, written in my worst hand so she would know it genuine: The love you gave was not wasted. It grew you. It remains in you. He was merely its occasion, not its cause.

Her sister wept when she read it. I do not know if she gave it.

Note: Hawthorn berries ready for picking. The old tree by the churchyard bears heavy this year.

For the Burning Last winter's snow

Fever's Dance

The little ones burn so hot, their bodies knowing what to do while we panic and cool and interfere. Fever is the fire that forges immunity, but it can also consume.

For the child whose fever rages:

  • Yarrow tea, to open the pores, to bring the heat to the surface
  • Elderflower, cooling from within, the tree's white gift
  • Strip to linen, sponge with water in which rose petals have floated
  • Watch the eyes. Consciousness is the keeper of the gate.

I lost one, years ago, before I learned to watch the eyes. She is buried where the yarrow grows thickest. I never pick from that patch. She tends it now, I think, in her way.

Note: Stock dried elderflower. The wet summer means mold in last year's harvest.

Written by candlelight in the cottage at the wood's edge
