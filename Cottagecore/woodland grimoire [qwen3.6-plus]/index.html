Chapter I

Potions & Elixirs

☽ ✦ ☾

Every potion begins with intention. Measure not only with spoons and scales, but with the quiet knowing in your bones. These are the recipes I have gathered through many seasons of trial, error, and the occasional explosion.

AUTUMN

Dreamweaver's Draught

✦ Gentle

For vivid dreams and restful slumber when the mind refuses to settle

Ingredients

  • 3 sprigs of lavender, dried under the waxing moon
  • 1 tsp mugwort, harvested at dusk
  • 2 tsp wildflower honey (from your own hives, if possible)
  • 1 cup moonwater (rainwater collected during the full moon)
  • A pinch of star anise, crushed between thumb and forefinger

Method

  1. Warm the moonwater in a copper pot — never let it boil, only simmer
  2. Add lavender and mugwort, stirring widdershins three times
  3. Whisper your intention into the steam
  4. Steep for seven minutes (no more, no less — patience is part of the magic)
  5. Strain through muslin, stir in honey until dissolved
  6. Add star anise as the final touch. Serve warm in your favourite cup

Witch's Note

Best consumed whilst sitting by the window, watching the rain. I keep a small bottle of this beside my bed for nights when the worries come uninvited. Do not give to children under seven — their dreams are already too large to manage.

WINTER

Hearthfire Tonic

✦✦ Moderate

To ward off winter's chill and fortify the spirit during the darkest months

Ingredients

  • 1 cup elderberries, foraged in late autumn
  • 2 inches fresh ginger root, sliced thin
  • ¼ tsp cayenne pepper (adjust according to your courage)
  • ½ cup raw honey, preferably buckwheat
  • 1 cinnamon stick, broken in half
  • 4 cups spring water

Method

  1. Bring water to a rolling boil with cinnamon and ginger
  2. Add elderberries, reduce heat, and simmer for 45 minutes
  3. The mixture should reduce by half — this is important
  4. Strain while still warm, pressing the berries firmly
  5. While still hot, stir in honey and cayenne
  6. Pour into sterilised bottles. Labels are essential — you'll forget which is which
  7. Store in a cool, dark place. Keeps until spring

Witch's Note

This recipe saved me through the Great Frost of '19, when the snow was so deep I couldn't reach the garden for a fortnight. Take one tablespoon daily, or two if you've been foolish enough to go out in wet socks. The cayenne will wake you up faster than any scolding.

SPRING

Gossamer Glow Elixir

✦✦✦ Advanced

For skin that catches the morning light and a spirit that remembers joy

Ingredients

  • ½ cup rosewater, distilled from your own roses
  • 3 tbsp chamomile infusion, cooled
  • 1 tsp aloe vera gel (fresh from the leaf, not from a tube)
  • 5 drops of jojoba oil
  • A single crushed pearl or mica powder (for the glow — entirely optional but lovely)

Method

  1. Combine rosewater and chamomile in a glass vial
  2. Add aloe vera slowly, shaking gently between additions
  3. Introduce jojoba oil drop by drop — this is not a step to rush
  4. If using pearl or mica, add last and swirl (do not shake) to create the shimmer
  5. Leave the vial on your windowsill for one full day to charge in sunlight
  6. Apply to pulse points before dawn for best results

Witch's Note

My grandmother taught me this one. She said it wasn't about vanity — it was about remembering that you are made of light, even on the days you forget. I make a fresh batch every spring equinox and keep it in a blue glass bottle on my dressing table.

SUMMER

Sun-Steeped Switchel

✦ Gentle

A cooling summer draught for gardeners, foragers, and anyone who works in the sun

Ingredients

  • 2 tbsp apple cider vinegar (with the mother, always)
  • 2 tbsp fresh mint leaves, bruised to release their oils
  • 1 tbsp honey, from summer blossoms
  • 1 tsp grated ginger
  • 1 litre cold well water
  • A handful of nettles, steeped separately and cooled

Method

  1. Steep nettles in hot water for 20 minutes, then cool completely
  2. Combine vinegar, honey, and ginger in a large jug
  3. Add the cooled nettle tea and remaining water
  4. Bruise the mint between your palms and add it whole
  5. Let sit for at least an hour. Better yet, leave it in the garden to steep in the sun
  6. Strain and serve over ice, with a sprig of mint for garnish

Witch's Note

This is my go-to for long summer days of weeding and planting. The nettles provide iron and minerals, the vinegar settles the stomach, and the mint cools you from the inside out. I keep a jug in the garden shed and another in the kitchen. The cat tries to drink it sometimes. I don't blame her.

Chapter II

The Pressed Garden

❀ ✦ ❁

Between these pages, I keep the garden as it was — pressed flat and preserved, each flower a small memory of a particular morning, a particular light. A pressed flower is a spell that holds time still.

Lavender

Lavandula angustifolia

Blooms: June – July Magic: Calming, Dreams, Protection Grows: Along the cottage wall, full sun

I press these between the pages of old telephone books — the ones nobody wants anymore. The purple fades to the softest grey-violet, but the scent remains if you press them thickly enough.

Chamomile

Matricaria chamomilla

Blooms: July – August Magic: Healing, Peace, Patience Grows: In the herb spiral, near the path

The smallest flowers hold the most comfort. I dry these on a linen cloth in the kitchen, turning them daily. They lose their cheerfulness when pressed — the white browns, the yellow darkens — but they still smell of apples and summer evenings.

Foxglove

Digitalis purpurea

Blooms: June Magic: Vision, Caution, The In-Between Grows: Wild at the wood's edge

⚠ Handle with care. Every part of this plant is poisonous. I press these for their beauty and their lesson — that the most striking things in nature demand the most respect. Keep away from the tea blends. (I learned this the hard way. Do not be me.)

Yarrow

Achillea millefolium

Blooms: July – September Magic: Protection, Healing, Courage Grows: In the meadow, everywhere really

Achilles used yarrow to staunch his soldiers' wounds — hence the name. I keep pressed yarrow in my first aid kit and my spell jar alike. It's one of those plants that does double duty: practical and magical, like a good pair of boots that also happens to be enchanted.

Wild Rose

Rosa canina

Blooms: June – October Magic: Love, Beauty, Divination Grows: Along the hedgerow, past the old gate

The wild roses by the gate are the best kind — not the fussy cultivated ones, but the ones that grow where they please and smell like they have secrets to tell. I press one petal from each bloom, not the whole flower. The whole flower is too proud to be pressed. The petals are humble enough.

Mugwort

Artemisia vulgaris

Blooms: August – September Magic: Dreams, Protection, Divination Grows: By the garden wall, self-seeded

Named for Artemis, goddess of the moon and the wild places. I hang bunches of dried mugwort above my door and under my pillow. The pressed specimens are for my dream journal — one leaf tucked into each entry, a record of what the night brought.

🌱 Spring Foraging

The earth wakes, and so must we

Nettles

Where: Rich soil, near old walls and paths

When: Early spring, before flowering

Use: Tea, soup, iron tonic

Wear gloves. Always wear gloves. I say this every year and still get stung every year.

Wild Garlic

Where: Damp woodland floors, in great drifts

When: April – May

Use: Pesto, infused oil, heart tonic

Follow your nose — you'll smell it before you see it. Don't confuse with lily of the valley (which is poisonous). If it doesn't smell like garlic, don't eat it.

Dandelion

Where: Everywhere. Absolutely everywhere.

When: March – October (leaves best in spring)

Use: Leaves in salad, flowers for wine, root for coffee substitute

The most underappreciated plant in the garden. Every part is useful. The children laugh when I harvest them. They won't be laughing when I serve dandelion wine at Midsummer.

Elderflower

Where: Hedgerows, field edges, garden margins

When: Late May – June

Use: Cordial, fritters, immune support

Harvest on a dry morning after the dew has lifted. Shake gently to evict any insects. They were there first, after all.

☀️ Summer Foraging

Abundance — gather wisely, store generously

Blackberry

Where: Hedgerows, bramble patches, everywhere

When: August – September

Use: Jams, syrups, wine, vinegar

The old saying: don't pick after Michaelmas (Sept 29). I say don't pick after the birds have had their fill. Leave some. Always leave some.

Meadowsweet

Where: Damp meadows, stream banks

When: June – August

Use: Tea for headaches, mead flavouring

Smells like almonds and honey. The flowers make a tea that eases pain — nature's aspirin, and far more pleasant.

Rose Hips

Where: Wherever wild roses grow

When: Late summer – autumn (after first frost is sweetest)

Use: Syrup, tea, vitamin C powerhouse

Remove the seeds and hairs carefully — they irritate the throat. The syrup alone is worth the fuss.

Plantain

Where: Paths, lawns, anywhere feet have trodden

When: All summer

Use: Poultice for stings and cuts, salad green

Chew a leaf and apply to nettle stings. Works in seconds. I carry a leaf in my pocket all summer, just in case.

🍂 Autumn Foraging

The great harvest — prepare for the dark months

Hawthorn Berries

Where: Hawthorn trees (naturally), hedgerows

When: September – November

Use: Heart tonic, jelly, protective charms

The hawthorn is the witch's tree. Never cut one down. Berries are mealy raw but make excellent medicine.

Sloe

Where: Blackthorn bushes, often with hawthorn

When: October – November (after first frost)

Use: Sloe gin, syrup, astringent

Freeze them first if frost hasn't arrived. The freezing bursts the skins and releases the flavour. My sloe gin from three years ago is still the best thing in my cupboard.

Mushrooms

Where: Ancient woodland, old pastures

When: September – November

Use: Food, tincture, tea (know your species!)

⚠ Never eat a mushroom you cannot identify with absolute certainty. When in doubt, leave it out. I carry three field guides and still sometimes leave things alone.

Acorns

Where: Beneath oak trees

When: September – October

Use: Flour (after leaching), coffee substitute, divination

Leach the tannins by soaking in water for several days, changing the water daily. Then dry and grind. Acorn flour makes dense, nutty bread that tastes like the forest floor (in a good way).

❄️ Winter Foraging

The earth sleeps — but the careful witch still finds

Ivy

Where: Trees, walls, hedgerows (evergreen)

When: All year, but best in winter when little else grows

Use: Respiratory remedy, protection

⚠ Berries are poisonous. Leaves are medicinal but must be prepared correctly. Do not confuse with other look-alikes.

Pine

Where: Coniferous woodland, parks

When: All year

Use: Tea (vitamin C), syrup, bath infusion

Pine needle tea is a winter staple. Young needles are best. The scent alone is enough to lift the winter blues.

Birch

Where: Birch woodland, moorland edges

When: Sap in late winter; bark and leaves all year

Use: Birch sap wine, diuretic tea, bark for kindling

Tap birch trees in late February for sap — but only if you know how to do it without harm. One tap per tree, and only from healthy specimens.

Stored Provisions

Where: Your own shelves, if you've been diligent

When: All winter

Use: Everything you dried, pickled, and preserved

This is why we work so hard in summer and autumn. The winter pantry is the witch's greatest achievement. Open each jar like a small miracle — because it is.

Chapter IV

Remedy Notes & Observations

✎ ✦ ✎

These are my notes — the messy, honest, unpolished thoughts that accumulate at the edges of practice. Not recipes, not quite. More like conversations with myself, in ink, on the good paper when I'm feeling generous.

15th March, 2024 ☁ Contemplative

The first day I truly felt spring in my bones today. Not on the calendar — in the bones. The air had that particular softness to it, like the world had been holding its breath and finally decided to exhale.

I made a simple tea of nettle and oat straw — nothing fancy, just what the body asked for after a long winter of heavy, warming things. The nettles are just coming up by the back wall, small and tender. I picked a handful and the sting felt almost like a greeting. "I'm back," they said. "Are you ready?"

I think I am. I've been stirring the compost, checking the seed trays, and — most importantly — opening the windows in the morning, even when it's still cold. The house needs to breathe. So do I.

Spring Nettle Oat Straw Journal
21st June, 2024 — Midsummer ☀ Joyful

Midsummer! The longest day and I spent most of it in the garden, as tradition demands. The lavender is at its peak — the bees know it too, the air above the plants is thick with them, humming like a choir.

I harvested three big bundles of lavender this evening, just as the sun was setting. The timing matters: the essential oils are strongest when the flowers are dry but the day's heat hasn't burned them off. I hung them in the kitchen to dry and the whole room smells like a Provençal dream.

Made a midsummer bonfire (small, controlled, responsible — I am a witch, not an arsonist). Burned some of last year's dried herbs as an offering and watched the smoke carry them away. There's something deeply satisfying about returning things to the air.

Summer Lavender Midsummer Journal
22nd September, 2024 — Equinox 🍂 Grateful

The equinox arrived with a crispness that caught me off guard. One day it was still summer-warm, the next — autumn. Proper autumn, with the smell of woodsmoke and the sound of geese overhead.

I spent the morning in the pantry, taking stock. Three jars of elderberry syrup, two of rose hip, a bottle of last year's sloe gin (the good one), dried chamomile in a tin, dried mint in another, and — I'm proud to say — the first batch of this year's meadowsweet, still fragrant.

I am grateful. Not in the abstract, Instagram-gratitude way, but in the deep, bone-level gratitude of someone who has watched seeds become plants become medicine become comfort. There is no feeling like it.

Tomorrow I'll go to the woods for mushrooms. Tonight I'll drink chamomile and listen to the wind change.

Autumn Elderberry Chamomile Equinox Journal
21st December, 2024 — Yule 🕯 Cozy

Yule. The shortest day, the longest night. I've lit every candle I own and it still feels like the darkness is pressing in at the windows. But that's the point, isn't it? This is the time for turning inward, for tending the small fires — literal and otherwise.

I made pine needle tea this afternoon and it tasted exactly like Christmas in a cup. The cat sat on my lap the entire time I was drinking it, which I took as a sign of approval.

I've been reading old herbals by the fire — Culpeper, mostly, and Gerard. It's remarkable how little has changed. The plants are the same. The needs are the same. The only thing that's different is that I have central heating and Gerard had to chop wood. I try not to feel guilty about this.

The solstice is a promise: the light returns. It always does. Until then, I have my candles, my cat, my books, and a very full pantry. I could ask for nothing more.

Quick Remedy: Tension Headache

1. Crush fresh peppermint leaves between your fingers

2. Inhale deeply three times

3. Apply remaining leaves to temples (avoid eyes!)

4. Lie down in a dark room with a cup of willow bark tea

5. Wait. Nature is not hurried, and neither should you be.

— From my personal notes, circa 2022

🤧

Quick Remedy: First Signs of a Cold

1. Make the strongest elderberry syrup tea you can manage

2. Add garlic (yes, really — one crushed clove)

3. Add honey and a squeeze of lemon

4. Drink it. Yes, even the garlic bit. Especially the garlic bit.

5. Go to bed. The internet will still be there tomorrow.

— The recipe that got me through winter 2023

😴

Quick Remedy: Can't Sleep

1. Warm milk (or oat milk) with nutmeg and honey

2. A few drops of lavender oil on your pillow

3. No screens. Read a book instead. Something boring is ideal.

4. Count your breaths, not sheep. In for 4, hold for 7, out for 8.

5. If still awake after 20 minutes, get up and make chamomile. Try again.

— Written at 3am, which proves I don't always follow my own advice
