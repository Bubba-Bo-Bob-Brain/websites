Photosynthetic Façades

Living building skins that convert solar energy, filter air, and produce biomass through integrated microalgae and vascular plant systems—transforming architecture into active ecological participants.

Biological Architecture

The modern photosynthetic façade represents the culmination of three decades of research into living building systems. Unlike early green walls that were essentially decorative, these integrated systems function as metabolic extensions of the structure itself—harvesting energy, processing waste, and producing useful biomass.

The typical system comprises three functional layers: an outer transparent biopolymer skin that modulates light transmission; a middle cultivation layer with nutrient delivery and gas exchange; and an inner thermal mass layer that stores harvested energy. This tripartite design echoes the structure of a leaf—cuticle, mesophyll, and vascular tissue—though engineers emphasize the convergent evolution rather than biomimicry per se.

Cultivation Methodologies

Two primary cultivation approaches have emerged: closed photobioreactor systems and open biological matrix systems. The former offer precise control and higher productivity but require more maintenance; the latter are more resilient and support polycultures but with variable yields.

Characteristic Photobioreactor Biological Matrix
Productivity High (controlled) Moderate (variable)
Biodiversity Monoculture typical Polyculture, self-balancing
Maintenance Regular intervention Self-regulating
Winter Performance Consistent with heating Dormancy, structural plants persist
Biomass Use Pharmaceutical grade Compost, feed, fiber
